About

A geometry found, not taught

My work sits at the intersection of optimal transport and reinforcement learning, focused on aggregation in decentralized policy training. When training spans distributed, asynchronous compute, the standard move, averaging parameters across nodes, implicitly treats policy space as Euclidean. It isn't. Treating it as a Riemannian manifold under the Wasserstein metric, and casting aggregation as a Schrödinger bridge rather than an arithmetic mean, gives principled aggregation rules with real convergence behavior under heterogeneity. The same machinery extends to generative modeling and biological transport, feeding a broader thesis: most of what looks like optimization noise is unaccounted-for geometry.

My work has moved from inferring single-cell transcriptomic trajectories underlying cell-fate branching in human brain organoids, drawing on Theoretical Frameworks and using optimal-transport-based trajectory inference in place of pseudotime heuristics, to modeling proliferating cell populations in cellular agriculture as unbalanced transport problems, developing the multi-scale foundation models behind Athenei Biosciences , and building the pretraining objectives and scalable architectures for sequential decision-making that anchor Physis Research .

Beyond this, I founded Imasion , a non-profit working to democratize the next generation of climate education in underserved communities in Peru, in partnership with MIT Solve, UN Women, Catalyst Now, Malala Fund, and others. It's the same conviction that runs through my technical work: that decentralizing access, whether to compute, to models, or to education, is what lets people historically locked out of a field actually contribute to it.

Research Focus

  1. Distributed policy optimization. Reframing distributed reinforcement learning as transport between probability distributions via Schrödinger bridges, replacing Euclidean parameter averaging with geometric aggregation on the Wasserstein manifold, directly relevant to aggregating policy updates across globally distributed, high-latency training nodes.
  2. Scalable optimal transport. Structured matrix approximations and hierarchical coupling via butterfly factorisation, making Wasserstein map computation tractable at scale, a building block toward lower-bandwidth alternatives to standard parameter synchronization in decentralized training.
  3. Geometric theory of learning dynamics. The JKO scheme as a lens on distributed learning, with variational optimality and population dynamics unfolding in Wasserstein space, casting decentralized aggregation as a gradient flow with real convergence implications for heterogeneous, asynchronous compute.
  4. Biological transport problems. Framing cellular agriculture and proliferating cell populations as optimal transport, where mass isn't conserved, solved via Schrödinger bridges, a separate line of work connecting the same geometric toolkit to biology.
