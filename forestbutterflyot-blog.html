The Forest Inside the Butterfly

A Wasserstein map is a dense object. Butterfly factorisation breaks it into a hierarchy of tiny, shallow trees. Drag the slider below and watch a dense coupling matrix turn into a forest.

Start with the problem ForestButterflyOT actually solves: computing an optimal transport map between two large distributions costs you O(n²) in the number of points, because the coupling matrix connecting source to target is, in the worst case, fully dense. Every point can potentially send mass to every other point.

That's fine at n = 100. It stops being fine once n reaches the size of a real single-cell dataset, or a real policy checkpoint. Something has to give — and the thing that gives, in ForestButterflyOT, is the assumption that the map needs to be dense at all.

A hierarchy instead of a matrix

Butterfly factorisation replaces one dense n × n matrix with a stack of O(log n) sparse layers, each one only connecting nearby nodes to a small number of "hub" nodes one level up. The name comes from the classic FFT butterfly diagram — connections cross in a distinctive X pattern between layers.

What I didn't appreciate until I drew this out by hand: that hierarchy is a forest. Each hub and its descendants form a shallow tree, and the whole factorisation is just several of those trees stitched together at the root layer. Move the slider and watch the trees appear:

Hover a node to trace which leaves it can reach. Each additional level roughly halves the connections needed per node — that's the log n scaling.

At depth 2, the savings look modest. By depth 5, at just 32 leaves, you're already discarding the majority of the dense matrix — and the gap widens every time n doubles. That's the whole argument, compressed into one slider.

Why "forest" and not just "tree"

A single butterfly factorisation is one tree of hubs. ForestButterflyOT uses several trees in parallel, each rooted at a different anchor point in the distribution, then reconciles their overlapping coverage. Picture it the way the illustration on this project's card does: multiple small dendritic structures, each covering its own neighbourhood, with a few long-range connections stitching the whole canopy together.

Sparsity isn't a compromise here — it's the correct prior. Optimal transport plans are, empirically, close to sparse already.

This matters for a very practical reason connected to the rest of my work: Wasserstein-geometric aggregation in decentralized RL (see WassersteinRL) needs to compute these maps repeatedly, across asynchronous, bandwidth-constrained nodes. A dense O(n²) map is not something you want to be shipping across a slow link every training step. A forest of shallow trees is.

Where this is going

The current ForestButterflyOT preprint establishes the factorisation and its approximation error bounds. The natural next step — and the one I'm most interested in — is using it as the communication primitive underneath WassersteinRL's aggregation step, so that geometric aggregation across distributed policy checkpoints stops paying the dense-matrix tax entirely.
