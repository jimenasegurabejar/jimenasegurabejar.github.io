The Forest Inside the Butterfly

A Wasserstein map is a dense object. Butterfly factorisation breaks it into a hierarchy of tiny, shallow trees. Drag the slider below and watch a dense coupling matrix turn into a forest.

Start with the problem ForestButterflyOT actually solves: computing an optimal transport map between two large distributions costs you O(n²) in the number of points, because the coupling matrix connecting source to target is, in the worst case, fully dense — every point can potentially send mass to every other point, and naively that means storing and manipulating n² numbers to represent a single map.

That's fine at n = 100. It stops being fine once n reaches the size of a real single-cell dataset — tens of thousands of cells — or a real distributed training run with hundreds of policy checkpoints exchanging updates every step. Something has to give, and the standard move in numerical linear algebra when a dense operator turns out to be unaffordable is to ask whether it has hidden structure. The claim underlying ForestButterflyOT is that Wasserstein couplings almost always do: the optimal transport plan concentrates its mass locally, and a matrix that is effectively sparse can be represented, manipulated, and applied far more cheaply than its dense form suggests.

A hierarchy instead of a matrix

Butterfly factorisation replaces one dense n × n matrix with a product of O(log n) sparse factors, each with O(n) nonzero entries — so the total storage and application cost falls from O(n²) to O(n log n). Concretely: each layer connects nearby nodes to a small number of "hub" nodes one level up, and applying the factorised map means passing information through log₂n such layers instead of touching all n² pairwise entries directly. The name comes from the classic FFT butterfly diagram — the same recursive structure Cooley and Tukey exploited to bring the discrete Fourier transform from O(n²) down to O(n log n); connections cross in the same distinctive X pattern between layers here.

What I didn't appreciate until I drew this out by hand: that hierarchy is a forest. Each hub and its descendants form a shallow tree, and the whole factorisation is just several of those trees stitched together at the root layer. Move the slider and watch the trees appear:

Why "forest" and not just "tree"

A single butterfly factorisation is one tree of hubs. ForestButterflyOT uses several trees in parallel, each rooted at a different anchor point in the distribution, then reconciles their overlapping coverage. Picture it the way the illustration on this project's card does: multiple small dendritic structures, each covering its own neighbourhood, with a few long-range connections stitching the whole canopy together.

Sparsity isn't a compromise here — it's the correct prior. Optimal transport plans are, empirically, close to sparse already.

This matters for a very practical reason connected to the rest of my work: Wasserstein-geometric aggregation in decentralized RL (see WassersteinRL) needs to compute these maps repeatedly, across asynchronous, bandwidth-constrained nodes. A dense O(n²) map is not something you want to be shipping across a slow link every training step. A forest of shallow trees is.

The price of compression

None of this is free. Forcing a coupling matrix through a hierarchy of shallow trees discards whatever mass genuinely needs to travel between two points that don't share a nearby hub — a long-range transport that the true optimal plan requires but the factorised approximation can't represent efficiently. The relevant question isn't whether error exists, but how it scales with depth and with the true sparsity of the underlying plan.

Higher concentration (mass genuinely stays local) means the butterfly approximation converges to the true plan faster as depth increases — this is the empirical regularity ForestButterflyOT's error bound formalises, not an assumption it requires you to make blindly.

In practice, real Wasserstein couplings — between single-cell states, or between policy checkpoints separated by one gradient step rather than a full retraining — sit well into the high-concentration regime, which is exactly why the approximation is usable rather than merely elegant.

Where this is going

The current ForestButterflyOT preprint establishes the factorisation and its approximation error bounds. The natural next step — and the one I'm most interested in — is using it as the communication primitive underneath WassersteinRL's aggregation step, so that geometric aggregation across distributed policy checkpoints stops paying the dense-matrix tax entirely.

Further reading

The butterfly factorisation pattern traces back to Cooley and Tukey's 1965 fast Fourier transform, and its modern generalisation to learned, data-adaptive structured matrices was formalised by Dao et al. (2019), "Learning Fast Algorithms for Linear Transforms Using Butterfly Factorizations." ForestButterflyOT adapts that structured-matrix machinery specifically to Wasserstein couplings, where the sparsity pattern is dictated by transport geometry rather than learned from scratch.
