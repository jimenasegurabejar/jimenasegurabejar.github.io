The Growth Curve Has a Dark Side

Every cellular agriculture pitch deck has the same S-curve: slow start, exponential rise, smooth plateau at capacity. That curve is real. It is also only one regime of a much richer dynamical system — and the same growth rate that makes it beautiful can make it oscillate, or worse, chaotic.

Cells dividing inside a bioreactor. Each division is simple. What the population does in aggregate is not always simple at all.

The starting point is the Verhulst logistic equation, first proposed in 1838 to correct the unbounded exponential growth model: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K). Population N grows at intrinsic rate r subject to a resource ceiling K — nutrient availability, dissolved oxygen, waste accumulation, physical space — at which growth self-limits. As a flow, the equation has a unique non-trivial fixed point at N* = K, and linearizing about it gives d(δN)/dt = −rδN: the perturbation decays exponentially, so N* is asymptotically stable for every r > 0. There is no parameter regime in which the continuous model does anything other than settle.

That last sentence is the entire reason the discrete version is worth taking seriously. Continuous time is a modeling convenience — real bioreactor operation is not continuous. Feed-batch and fed-batch protocols add nutrient and remove product on a cycle: daily, or every few generations. The natural object for a system updated once per cycle is not a flow but a map, and the qualitative behavior of maps and flows near a fixed point can differ in a way that has nothing to do with modeling error and everything to do with the mathematics of iteration itself.

Nothing about this model predicts trouble. Trouble shows up when you stop modeling time continuously and start modeling it the way a real bioreactor actually runs: in discrete batches, feeding and harvesting on a cycle rather than continuously. That turns the same growth law into a map instead of a flow — and maps can do things flows can't.

Why iteration is different from flow

The discrete-time analogue is x_{n+1} = f(x_n) = rx_n(1 − x_n), with x rescaled to [0,1] as a fraction of carrying capacity. It has the same non-trivial fixed point condition, x* = f(x*), giving x* = 1 − 1/r for r > 1. But stability of a fixed point under iteration is governed by a different criterion than stability of a flow: x* is stable if and only if |f'(x*)| < 1. Computing the derivative, f'(x*) = 2 − r, so the stability condition becomes 1 < r < 3.

At r = 3 exactly, |f'(x*)| = 1 — the fixed point loses stability through a period-doubling bifurcation. Past that threshold, the population no longer converges to a single value; it settles into a stable 2-cycle, alternating between two population levels forever. The same mechanism recurs: each new cycle is itself a fixed point of the twice-iterated map f∘f, and it too eventually loses stability as r increases, doubling the period again. The sequence of thresholds — 3, 3.449, 3.544, 3.564... — accumulates geometrically, converging to the Feigenbaum point r_∞ ≈ 3.56995, beyond which the attractor is, for almost every parameter value, chaotic. This is not a metaphorical use of the word: trajectories exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions, positive topological entropy, and a positive Lyapunov exponent — the same formal criteria used to classify chaos in any dynamical system, biological or otherwise.

A continuous system settles down. Its discrete cousin, run too aggressively, can bifurcate its way into chaos using the exact same growth rate.

The discrete version breaks differently

Replace the flow with the discrete logistic map, x_{n+1} = r · x_n(1 − x_n), and watch what happens as r crosses a series of thresholds. Below r ≈ 3: a stable population, cycle after cycle. Between roughly 3 and 3.57: the population starts alternating between two, then four, then eight values — period doubling. Past r ≈ 3.57: chaos. Same equation. Same biological growth rate. Wildly different outcome.

This is not a purely academic warning. A batch-fed bioreactor scaled up carelessly — too much nutrient added per cycle relative to the population's actual carrying capacity — moves its effective r to the right along that bifurcation diagram without anyone deciding to do so. What looks like "the culture is being temperamental" is frequently a system that has quietly crossed into a periodic or chaotic regime.

Sensitive dependence, made visible

The formal signature of chaos is a positive Lyapunov exponent: two trajectories started arbitrarily close together separate at an exponential rate, |δx_n| ≈ |δx_0|e^{λn}, with λ > 0. Below the chaotic threshold, by contrast, nearby trajectories converge — λ < 0 — which is exactly why the stable and periodic regimes are experimentally reproducible and the chaotic one is not.

Two batch cultures, started a fraction of a percent apart in initial density, run under identical conditions. In the stable and periodic regimes they stay indistinguishable. In the chaotic regime, an immeasurably small difference in starting density becomes the entire story within a few dozen cycles — a direct experimental consequence of λ > 0, not measurement noise.

For a process engineer, the practical content of a positive Lyapunov exponent is blunt: in that regime, no amount of improved initial-condition measurement will make the batch outcome predictable, because prediction error grows exponentially with the number of cycles regardless of how precisely the starting state is known. The only lever that helps is moving r itself back below threshold.

Where this connects

This dynamical-systems view is the scaffolding underneath ProlifOT, which frames the same proliferating populations as an unbalanced optimal transport problem — mass is created, not just moved. The dynamical systems picture here explains why that framing has to allow for regime changes rather than assuming a population is always cleanly converging: any transport-based model of a bioreactor population has to know which regime it's actually in before it can say anything useful about where the mass is going next.

Further reading

The logistic equation originates with Pierre-François Verhulst (1838). The discrete map's route to chaos, and its relevance to population biology specifically, was made precise by Robert May's 1976 Nature paper "Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics" — still the standard entry point for this material, and the reason the discrete logistic map is a fixture of every dynamical systems course rather than a curiosity.
